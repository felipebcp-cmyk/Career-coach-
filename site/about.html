About

Nobody ever told me my work was wrong.
It was worse — it was unused.

I'm Felipe. I built the Playbook because for years I was the most rigorous person in the building and the least consulted — and because the fix, once I found it, turned out to be teachable.

If you work in finance, I probably don't need to describe your month to you. You already know the 11pm close. The pack that ships at 8am with every variance explained to two decimal places. The three people who open it. The zero who act on it. I lived that month, on repeat, for [X] years across [audit and industry finance roles] — and by every technical measure, I was good. Clean workings, defensible numbers, deadlines met.

Which is exactly why it took me so long to notice the problem.

The corridor

The moment I can't forget: I'd spent two weeks building the analysis for a capacity investment — sensitivity tables, scenarios, the lot. Walking to the meeting where it would be presented, I passed the divisional GM and the ops director in the corridor, mid-conversation. By the time we sat down, the decision was made. My analysis was "noted." Sixteen pages, noted.

I did what most of us do: I blamed the audience. They don't read. They've already decided. Finance isn't respected here. It took a mentor one sentence to dismantle all of it:

"You answered every question except the one that mattered: what should we do?"

She was right. I had never once, in years of analysis, committed to a recommendation. I presented options and hid behind them. I sent packs instead of having conversations. I spoke in basis points to people who thought in trucks, shifts and customers. I was renting out a calculator and wondering why I wasn't treated like an advisor.

The rebuild

So I studied the people who were treated like advisors — the finance people leaders pulled into rooms before decisions. What I found was almost insulting in its simplicity: they weren't better at finance than me. They did a different job with the same numbers. They led with the answer. They socialised big ideas one-to-one before any meeting. They translated "EBITDA margin compression from adverse mix" into "we're selling more of the products we make less money on." They priced "do nothing." They said "here's what I'd do, and here's what would change my mind."

None of it was talent. All of it was technique. I turned those techniques into checklists and forced myself through them — the stakeholder map, the headline-first commentary, the one-page recommendation, the pre-mortem. Within [a year], the calls started coming before the decisions. The corridor conversations started including me. Eventually, [the role/title] followed.

Why I teach it

Because the gap that held me back is the same gap holding back thousands of technically excellent finance people — and it is never taught. Your degree taught you to be right. Your firm taught you to be fast. Nobody teaches the third thing: how to make being right matter. That's the whole Playbook — every framework that moved me, in the order they moved me, with workshops that make you run them on your own numbers, your own stakeholders, your own next meeting.

Two things I want you to know about how I built it. First, there's no filler — thirteen lessons of about twenty minutes, because your evenings are already spoken for. Second, the capstone is deliberately uncomfortable: a business whose good news is hiding a bad decision, a GM who doesn't want to hear it, and one page to change their mind. If a course about influence ends in a multiple-choice victory lap, it has lied to you.

The human bit

Outside the spreadsheets: [I live in — city], [personal detail — family, the dog, the football team that disappoints you annually]. I still keep the sixteen-page "noted" analysis in a drawer — not as a wound, but as a reminder of the difference between being thorough and being heard. The drawer also contains the one-pager that replaced it. Guess which one got funded.

If you're where I was — respected enough to be kept, not yet trusted enough to be asked — I built this for you. Seven weeks. Your numbers. A bigger seat.
